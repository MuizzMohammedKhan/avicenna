I · Origins

The Making of a Mind

c. 980 — Afshana, near Bukhara

Born in the Samanid World

Abū ʿAlī al-Ḥusayn ibn ʿAbd Allāh ibn Sīnā was born in the village of Afshana, near Bukhara, then the capital of the Samanid Empire — a Persianate dynasty ruling over parts of Central Asia and northeastern Iran.

His father served as governor of one of the estates of the Samanid ruler Nūḥ ibn Manṣūr. The family home was a meeting place for men of learning, and young Ibn Sina absorbed scholarship from an early age. By ten, he had memorized the Qurʾān and much of Arabic poetry.

c. 990 — Bukhara

Education at the Court Library

His family moved to Bukhara, where he studied Hanafi jurisprudence, arithmetic, geometry, astronomy, logic, and philosophy. He mastered logic, natural philosophy, and medicine while still a teenager.

A decisive moment came when he cured the Samanid ruler Nūḥ ibn Manṣūr of a serious illness. As a reward, he was granted access to the royal library — a treasure house of Greek, Persian, and Arabic manuscripts that shaped his entire intellectual synthesis.

c. 997–1000

Mastery by Sixteen

By his own account, he had mastered physics, mathematics, logic, and metaphysics before sixteen, and began practicing medicine. He wrote later that medicine was "no hard and thorny science" compared to mathematics and metaphysics.

c. 990s

First Writings

His earliest known work, the Compendium on the Soul, was dedicated to the Samanid ruler. At twenty-one, he composed Philosophy for ʿArūḍī — a comprehensive survey of all philosophy except mathematics.

c. 1000

The Library Opened

Access to the Samanid royal library allowed him to study Aristotle, the Neoplatonists, and earlier Islamic thinkers in depth. This formed the basis of his later synthesis.

"The knowledge of anything, since all things have causes, is not acquired or complete unless its causes are known." Ibn Sina
II · The Turning Point

A Life Set Adrift

c. 1000–1002

The Fall of the Samanids

The Turkic Qarakhanids ended Samanid rule in 999. Around the same time, his father died. Without a patron or his father's support, Ibn Sina left Bukhara — "necessity led me to forsake Bukhara" — and entered a life of wandering.

He traveled through Gurganj (Khwarazm), Nishapur, and Gurgan, acting as physician and administrator by day while gathering students for philosophical discussion each evening. Despite chaos, he continued producing top-quality scholarship.

c. 1012–1015

Rayy, Hamadan, and the Canon

He moved to Rayy near present-day Tehran, then to Hamadan, where he became court physician to the Buyid ruler Shams al-Dawla. He began work on the Canon of Medicine — his medical masterpiece — and met his lifelong disciple and scribe, al-Jūzjānī.

In Hamadan, he twice served as vizier. Politics was perilous: he was forced into hiding by his opponents and spent time as a political prisoner. After Shams al-Dawla's death in 1021, he sought new patronage.

c. 1022–1025

The Escape to Isfahan

Imprisoned and fearing for his life, Ibn Sina fled Hamadan disguised as a Sufi ascetic, accompanied by his brother, a student, and two slaves. He reached Isfahan and was warmly welcomed by the Kakuyid ruler ʿAlāʾ al-Dawla.

His final years brought comparative peace. He served as chief physician and counselor, completed major works begun in Hamadan, and accompanied the ruler on military campaigns — during which many of his later writings were composed.

"I prefer a short life with width to a narrow one with length." Ibn Sina
III · The Courts

Patronage and Peril

A Life Between Courts

Ibn Sina's adult life unfolded across competing dynasties — each court a world of opportunity and danger. He moved from patron to patron, serving as physician, advisor, and sometimes vizier.

The Vizier

As vizier to Shams al-Dawla in Hamadan, Ibn Sina combined medical practice with administrative and political responsibility. He reflected deeply on ethics, politics, and practical wisdom — themes woven into his practical philosophy.

The Companion

Under ʿAlāʾ al-Dawla in Isfahan, he found his most stable patronage. He accompanied the ruler on campaigns, composed works in camp, and enjoyed the intellectual companionship of a court that valued learning.

IV · The System

A Philosophy of Everything

Ibn Sina sought to integrate all aspects of science and religion into a grand metaphysical vision — explaining the formation of the universe, the problems of evil, prayer, providence, prophecy, miracles, and the ultimate destiny of humanity.

Essence & Existence

He distinguished quiddity (what a thing is) from existence (that it is). We can know what a thing is without knowing whether it exists. This distinction underlies his entire modal ontology.

The Necessary Existent

His proof of God: the aggregate of all contingent things must itself have a cause that is not contingent — a Necessary Existent whose essence is identical to its existence. God is the only being that exists by necessity.

The Flying Man

A thought experiment: a man created floating in air, deprived of all senses. He would still affirm "I exist" — proving the soul is immaterial and self‑aware independent of sensory experience. A precursor to Descartes' Cogito.

Active Intellect

Following Aristotle and al‑Fārābī, he posited an Active Intellect that actualizes human thought. The human intellect is potential until illuminated by this celestial agent, which provides the forms we abstract from experience.

Science & Religion

He offered rational accounts of prophecy, miracles, and revelation within his metaphysical framework, arguing that philosophy and revealed religion are compatible — both lead to the same truth.

Logic as Instrument

Logic was the foundation of his scientific method — the tool through which philosophy could systematically investigate reality. He reorganized the Aristotelian Organon into a science of second intentions.

"The world is a stream of events, and the philosopher is the one who sees the necessity flowing through it." Ibn Sina
V · The Written World

A Corpus of Genius

Ibn Sina wrote nearly 100 works — philosophical encyclopedias, medical treatises, and concise summaries. His writings shaped intellectual life for centuries across the Islamic, Byzantine, and Latin worlds.

The Book of Healing (Kitāb al-Shifāʾ)

A massive philosophical encyclopedia covering logic, physics, mathematics, and metaphysics — his magnum opus in philosophy.

Composed over many years, it systematically presents his entire philosophical system. The Latin translation of parts of it (the Liber Sextus Naturalium) influenced medieval scholasticism.

The Canon of Medicine (Al-Qānūn fī al-Ṭibb)

His medical masterpiece. Translated to Latin in the 12th century, it became the standard textbook in European medical schools, consulted well into the 17th century.

Divided into five books: general principles, materia medica, diseases of organs, diseases of the whole body, and compound drugs. It described 760 drugs and introduced systematic clinical trials.

Philosophy for ʿArūḍī (GS 2)

A comprehensive work on all philosophy, written at the request of a neighbor — an early statement of his systematic vision.

Covers logic, natural philosophy, mathematics, and metaphysics, but explicitly excludes mathematics (which he treated separately). It shows his ambition to present philosophy as an ordered whole.

The Available and the Valid (GS 10)

Twenty-volume commentaries on the works of Aristotle — engaging directly with the philosopher who shaped his entire approach.

These commentaries, now largely lost, were a major vehicle for transmitting Aristotelian thought to the Islamic and later Latin worlds. They show Avicenna's critical method.

Piety and Sin (GPP 1)

A two-volume work on the practical sciences — ethics applied to human conduct and society.

Explores the nature of the good, the classification of virtues, and the role of reason in moral life. Part of his broader practical philosophy alongside politics and household management.

The Book of Knowledge for ʿAlāʾ (Danishnama)

A Persian summa of philosophy written for his Isfahani patron — a concise, accessible overview of his system.

Notable for creating new scientific vocabulary in Persian. Covers logic, metaphysics, music theory, and other sciences. It demonstrates his ability to communicate complex ideas to a broader audience.

VI · The Healer

Medical Innovations

The Canon of Medicine was not merely a compilation — it introduced original contributions that shaped medicine for centuries. Here are some of his key discoveries and methods.

Contagious Diseases

He recognized the contagious nature of tuberculosis (phthisis) and other infectious diseases, and described how diseases spread through water and soil.

Meningitis

He provided the first known description of meningitis, distinguishing it from infectious brain disease and noting it could be caused by infection elsewhere in the body.

Pulse Diagnosis

He refined Galen's pulse theory, correctly describing the pulse as alternating expansion and contraction. He pioneered wrist pulse examination and identified over 50 distinct pulse types.

Pharmacology

He catalogued 760 drugs with detailed comments on their properties and effectiveness. He introduced systematic experimentation and quantification into pharmacology.

Clinical Trials

He advocated testing new drugs on animals and humans before general use — a proto‑clinical trial method. He also described the healing properties of gaseous mercury and recommended ice for fevers.

Surgical Techniques

He detailed procedures for intubation, tracheotomy, and bone setting. He designed catheters with rounded tips and side holes, and emphasized cleanliness before surgery.

Neuroscience

He discovered the cerebellar vermis and the caudate nucleus. He linked intellectual dysfunction to the brain's middle ventricle and recognized the frontal lobe's role in reasoning.

Psychosomatic Medicine

He diagnosed "love sickness" by pulse changes and linked emotional states to physical health. He described melancholia, mania, epilepsy, and other neuropsychiatric conditions.

The Islamic World

Avicenna's system dominated intellectual life across the Islamic world, sparking reactions from enthusiastic acceptance to rigorous refutation. He was called al-shaykh al-raʾīs — "The Preeminent Master" — second only to Aristotle in influence.

Medieval and Renaissance Europe

Beginning in the 12th century, Latin translations of his philosophy profoundly influenced scholastic thought. His metaphysics and theory of the soul shaped the arguments of Thomas Aquinas, Duns Scotus, and many others.

Medicine and Science

The Canon of Medicine standardized medical terminology and classification, transmitted Galenic and Aristotelian medicine in a unified framework, and advanced pharmacology and clinical reasoning. He described 760 drugs and recognized the contagious nature of tuberculosis.

12th c.
Latin Translation Begins
Gerard of Cremona translates the Canon and other works into Latin in Toledo. Avicenna's philosophy enters European universities.
13th–15th c.
Medical Textbook
The Canon becomes the primary textbook in European medical schools, used alongside Hippocrates and Galen.
16th–17th c.
Gradual Decline
New anatomical discoveries (e.g., Harvey's circulation) begin to supersede some of his teachings, but the Canon is still consulted.
Today
Enduring Legacy
Revered as the father of early modern medicine and a central figure in Islamic philosophy. His thought continues to be studied and debated.
"Medicine is a science from which one learns the states of the human body with respect to what is healthy and what is not, in order to preserve health when it exists and restore it when it is lost." The Canon of Medicine

Death in Campaign

Ibn Sina died in June 1037 in Hamadan, while accompanying ʿAlāʾ al-Dawla on a military campaign. He was approximately 57 years old. Despite applying his medical skills to himself, he succumbed to illness — reportedly chronic intestinal disease, possibly aggravated by over‑medication.

His autobiography, dictated to his student al‑Jūzjānī, remains our most extensive source on his life — a remarkable self‑portrait of a mind perpetually in motion.
